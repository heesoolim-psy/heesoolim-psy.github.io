About

I am a master’s student in the Social & Cultural Psychology Lab at Yonsei University, advised by Young-Hoon Kim. My research addresses a classic yet still central question in relationship science: what enables romantic relationships to flourish and to be maintained over time? Rather than asking only which individual characteristics predict relationship satisfaction, I ask with whom, when, and under what conditions they do so.

To date, my work has centered on three questions. First, how do the two partners’ characteristics and behaviors combine in their associations with relationship satisfaction? I have examined actor and partner effects and the balance between partners, asking whether what matters is a person’s own level, the partner’s level, or how the two stand relative to each other, for characteristics and behaviors ranging from attachment anxiety and avoidance to the share of sexual initiation each spouse takes on. Second, how does the importance of those characteristics vary across time and context? Couples move through stages, and the same characteristic can matter more at one point than at another. So far I have examined the transition to marriage, asking whether the partner qualities tied to relationship and sexual satisfaction before the wedding, such as personality, appearance, and socioeconomic status, are still the ones that matter afterward, and I am interested in extending this to other contexts that surround a couple. Third, how do partners perceive each other? I have compared self-reports with partner reports of personality and examined perceptions of a partner’s mate value, asking whose view carries more weight when the two diverge and whether the answer differs for women and men. I am particularly interested in the conditions under which a partner’s favorable view benefits relationship satisfaction more than one’s own.

My work draws mainly on dyadic longitudinal studies that follow Korean couples across the transition to marriage, two cohorts and more than 1,200 couples in total, and earlier on meta-analytic data. Methodologically, I specialize in dyadic and longitudinal analysis, including the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model, dyadic response surface analysis, structural equation modeling, and multilevel modeling. Most recently, I have begun to explore how factors at different levels, from the broader environment surrounding a couple down to the relationship itself, the self, and the partner, each contribute to couples’ relationship satisfaction.

Publications

Under Review

  1. Shin, J. E., Lim, H., & Kim, Y. H. (under review). How I see myself or how you see me: Gender differences in the relative weight of self- and partner-perceptions for relationship well-being.
    Summary & figures

    Question. When a person’s view of their own personality and their partner’s view of it diverge, whose view is more closely tied to relationship satisfaction, and is the answer the same for women and men? Earlier studies entered self- and partner-perceptions as parallel predictors, which cannot show what happens when the two conflict.

    Data and approach. 501 engaged heterosexual couples in South Korea, all planning to marry within six months, rated their own and their partner’s Big Five traits. Dyadic response surface analysis modeled relationship satisfaction across every combination of self- and partner-perception, and the slope along the line of incongruence (a₃) tested whether the direction of a discrepancy mattered.

    Findings. When the two views agreed, satisfaction followed how favorable they were, for both genders. When they diverged, the genders split. Women were more satisfied when their partner saw them more favorably than they saw themselves, for conscientiousness, openness, and extraversion. Men were more satisfied when their self-view was the more favorable one, for conscientiousness, openness, and neuroticism. Agreeableness was the exception: discrepancies in a woman’s agreeableness were related to her partner’s satisfaction rather than her own. Discrepancies are therefore not harmful in themselves; their direction is what matters.

In Preparation

  1. Lim, H., & Kim, Y. H. (in preparation). Beyond Who Initiates: The Division of Sexual Initiation and Sexual Satisfaction in Married Couples.
    Summary & figures

    Question. Research on couples’ sexual lives usually asks how often sex happens. Does it also matter how the initiating is divided, and is initiating more always better for the person who does it?

    Data and approach. 591 married couples from two cohorts. Each spouse independently divided 100% of sexual initiation between themselves and their partner; husbands claimed 64% on average and wives 39%, and the two reports summed to within ten points of 100 for 69% of couples. A curvilinear Actor–Partner Interdependence Model with linear and quadratic terms (robust ML) predicted each spouse’s sexual satisfaction, controlling for both partners’ age and sexual desire, marriage duration, cohort, and the couple’s sexual frequency.

    Findings. The association was an inverted U. Sexual satisfaction was highest when one’s own share of initiation was around a third and declined as initiation fell almost entirely to one person. The curve did not differ between husbands and wives and appeared only for one’s own share; the partner’s share showed no curvature. The pattern held whether the share was treated as eleven ordered bins (p = .005) or as a continuous score (p = .008), and it reappeared in simple individual-level regressions. The data are cross-sectional, so these are associations.

  2. Lim, H., Moon, H., Oh, C., & Kim, Y. H. (in preparation). Dating Partner or Marriage Partner? Premarital Perceptions of Partner Warmth, Attractiveness, and Resources as Predictors of Relationship Satisfaction Before and After Marriage.
    Summary & figures

    Question. Mate value is commonly described in three domains: personality, physical appearance, and socioeconomic status. Which of them is most closely tied to relationship satisfaction, and does the answer change once couples marry?

    Data and approach. Engaged couples from two cohorts (1,233 couples pooled) rated their partner in the three domains before marriage and reported their relationship satisfaction before and after the wedding. Actor–Partner Interdependence Models estimated concurrent associations and, controlling for earlier satisfaction, change over time, with cohabitation and cohort as covariates.

    Findings (preliminary). Before marriage, how people rated their partner’s personality was tied to their own satisfaction about three to four times as strongly as how they rated their partner’s appearance, and perceived socioeconomic status barely registered. Into marriage, personality continued to predict later satisfaction for both spouses, appearance held only for husbands, and socioeconomic status began to matter, most clearly for wives.

In the figures, raspberry marks women and teal marks men.
