About

I am a master’s student in the Social & Cultural Psychology Lab at Yonsei University, supervised by Young-Hoon Kim. My research is motivated by a long-standing question in relationship science: what allows romantic relationships to remain satisfying over time? Beyond asking which individual characteristics predict relationship satisfaction, I ask with whom, when, and under what conditions they matter.

My work so far has addressed three questions. First, how do partners’ characteristics combine in their associations with satisfaction? I have examined actor and partner effects, asking whether a person’s own level, the partner’s level, or the balance between the two matters, for characteristics ranging from attachment to sexual initiation. Second, how does the importance of those characteristics change over time and across people? The same characteristic can carry more weight at one point in a relationship than at another, and for one person than for another. I have examined whether the mate value people perceive in their partner (personality, appearance, and socioeconomic status) matters differently in dating and in marriage, and for women and for men. Beyond these two conditions, I am interested in the settings couples live in, such as their social networks, economic conditions, and cultural norms. Third, how do partners perceive each other? I have compared how people rate their own personality with how their partner rates it. I am particularly interested in the tension between these two views: when does a partner’s view matter more for satisfaction, and when does one’s own?

My work draws on two dyadic longitudinal studies that follow more than 1,200 Korean couples across the transition to marriage, as well as a survey of married couples. Methodologically, I specialize in dyadic and longitudinal analysis, including the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model, dyadic response surface analysis, structural equation modeling, and multilevel modeling. Most recently, I have begun to ask why the importance of these characteristics differs by relationship stage and gender, examining how factors across individual, relational, and environmental levels shape these differences.

Publications

Under Review

  1. Shin, J. E., Lim, H., & Kim, Y. H. (under review). How I see myself or how you see me: Gender differences in the relative weight of self- and partner-perceptions for relationship well-being.
    Summary & figures

    Question. When a person’s view of their own personality and their partner’s view of it diverge, whose view is more closely tied to relationship satisfaction, and is the answer the same for women and men? Earlier studies entered self- and partner-perceptions as parallel predictors, which cannot show what happens when the two conflict.

    Data and approach. 501 engaged heterosexual couples in South Korea, all planning to marry within six months, rated their own and their partner’s Big Five traits. Dyadic response surface analysis modeled relationship satisfaction across every combination of self- and partner-perception, and the slope along the line of incongruence (a₃) tested whether the direction of a discrepancy mattered.

    Findings. When the two views agreed, satisfaction followed how favorable they were, for both genders. When they diverged, the genders split. Women were more satisfied when their partner saw them more favorably than they saw themselves, for conscientiousness, openness, and extraversion. Men were more satisfied when their self-view was the more favorable one, for conscientiousness, openness, and neuroticism. Agreeableness was the exception: discrepancies in a woman’s agreeableness were related to her partner’s satisfaction rather than her own. Discrepancies are therefore not harmful in themselves; their direction is what matters.

In Preparation

  1. Lim, H., & Kim, Y. H. (in preparation). Beyond Who Initiates: The Division of Sexual Initiation and Sexual Satisfaction in Married Couples.
    Summary & figures

    Question. Research on couples’ sexual lives usually asks how often sex happens. Does it also matter how the initiating is divided, and is initiating more always better for the person who does it?

    Data and approach. Cross-sectional data were drawn from 591 heterosexual married couples in South Korea. Each spouse reported their own percentage share of the couple’s sexual initiation and their sexual satisfaction. An actor–partner interdependence model estimated linear and quadratic associations of each spouse’s initiation share with both spouses’ sexual satisfaction. Models adjusted for both spouses’ sexual desire, relationship satisfaction, and age, as well as marriage duration, couple sexual frequency, and cohort.

    Findings. Husbands reported larger initiation shares than wives. Yet both husbands and wives reported greater sexual satisfaction when initiation was less concentrated in one spouse: sexual satisfaction was relatively higher at intermediate own shares and lower toward more extreme shares. No corresponding association emerged between a spouse’s initiation share and their partner’s sexual satisfaction. More broadly, the findings shift attention from who initiates to how the initiating role is distributed within the couple.

  2. Lim, H., Moon, H., Oh, C., & Kim, Y. H. (in preparation). Dating Partner or Marriage Partner? The Impact of Perceived Partner Personality, Appearance, and Socioeconomic Status on Relationship Satisfaction Across the Transition to Marriage.
    Summary & figures

    Question. It is often said that the person you date and the person you marry call for different qualities. Mate value is commonly described in terms of three domains: personality, physical appearance, and socioeconomic status. Do these domains matter differently for relationship satisfaction before and after marriage?

    Data and approach. Engaged couples from two cohorts were followed from before marriage to one year after; analyses used the 634 couples who had married by follow-up. Each partner rated the other’s personality, appearance, and socioeconomic status before marriage, and the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model compared the three domains before and after marriage, separately for wives and husbands. The postmarital analysis controlled for premarital satisfaction.

    Findings (preliminary). Before marriage, both personality and appearance were associated with relationship satisfaction for women and men, with personality showing the stronger association, whereas socioeconomic status was not. One year into marriage, personality remained associated with satisfaction for both spouses, appearance remained so only for husbands, and socioeconomic status became relevant for both.
